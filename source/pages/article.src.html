LLM Evaluation Discrepancies And How To Prevent Them

One model on one benchmark is published at 34.10 by a leaderboard and at 68.20 by its developers. We look at what goes into a benchmark score, how much of it survives publication, and which fields a published result would need to carry.

Gemma-2-9B has more than one public score on BIG-Bench Hard (BBH). The Open LLM Leaderboard v2 lists 34.10. The model's own technical report gives 68.20. The two numbers are 34 percentage points (pp) apart, and neither is wrong.68.20 is exactly twice 34.10. That is a coincidence: the leaderboard's unrounded value is 34.097, and no step in either procedure doubles or halves a score.

The leaderboard ranks a fixed set of answer choices by how likely the model finds each one, then rescales the result. The report lets the model write out its reasoning and extracts the answer from what it wrote. When we re-ran the leaderboard's configuration we got 53.88 against its unrescaled 53.77, so nothing failed to reproduce. Each score can be reproduced under its own procedure. They cannot be meaningfully compared, and nothing published alongside either number tells you so.

This article is about that missing information. We start by taking a score apart: how a model's answer to one question becomes a number, and which steps along the way can change it, using the Gemma scores as the running example. We then follow other large disagreements between sources back to their causes, and look at 46,559 published evaluation records from 12 sources to count how many of those steps they record. We end with EvalSpec, a short list of fields we propose every published score should carry.

In the figures, blue marks answers obtained by likelihood ranking and gold answers obtained by generation. A dashed outline marks a detail of the procedure that the record in question does not carry.

A benchmark is a set of questions with reference answers, and a score is the share a model gets right. Scores are usually published with two labels attached, the model and the benchmark. The figure follows one question from the benchmark to the published number, once for each of the two Gemma scores. The lines split at the steps where the two sources are known to differ.

The steps in the middle are carried out by a piece of software called an evaluation harness: a bundle of prompt templates, a way of choosing worked examples, a scoring rule and an answer parser, each with defaults. Widely used harnesses include lm-evaluation-harness, HELM, LightEval and OpenCompass, and many model developers use pipelines of their own. We will call one published score, together with whatever its source says about how it was obtained, an evaluation record.

From an answer to a number

The harness wraps each question in a prompt template, optionally preceded by a few worked examples (their number is the shot count) and by an instruction to reason step by step (chain-of-thought). Earlier work has shown that scores and even model rankings shift with the harness, the prompt format and the way the answer is extracted. The step with the least visibility is the one after the prompt: how an answer is obtained from the model and marked. We call it the scoring mode.

Two ways to get an answer from a model

Take a multiple-choice question. One way to score it never asks the model to write anything. The harness appends each answer option to the prompt in turn, asks the model how probable that continuation is, and takes the most probable option as the answer. This is log-likelihood scoring. It always yields an answer, so a model with no idea still scores at the level of chance.

The other way is generation. The model writes free text, perhaps several sentences of reasoning, and a second piece of code, the answer parser, searches that text for the answer. Two things now have to go right: the model has to reach the right answer, and the parser has to find it. If the parser finds nothing, the item is marked wrong, so the floor is zero, below chance.

Nothing forces the two modes to agree. A model can rank the options one way and argue its way to another answer when allowed to write. Which mode is closer to what you care about depends on the benchmark. BBH collects tasks that take several steps of reasoning, and it was designed for three-shot chain-of-thought generation, which is how the Gemma report scores it. A model that can work such a task through in writing does not necessarily put the highest probability on the right option when it has to choose in a single pass. The leaderboard scores BBH by likelihood ranking. That is a legitimate choice, and it measures something different.

There are two more scoring modes. A human or model judge can rate the output, which we come to below, or the output can be executed against tests, as in code benchmarks.

The number shown may not be the number measured

A second step separates the leaderboard's 34.10 from what its harness measured. A model that guesses at random on four-option questions scores 25%, so the leaderboard subtracts that floor and stretches what remains back to a 0 to 100 scale. Its documentation gives the rule: subtract the random-guess baseline, divide by the range above it, and multiply by 100. The two starting points in the figure are Gemma-2-9B's own values on two other benchmarks, where the leaderboard's data has both the raw and the displayed number.

BBH is made of subtasks with different numbers of answer options. According to the same documentation, benchmarks with subtasks are rescaled subtask by subtask and then averaged, so no single baseline turns one BBH number into the other. For Gemma-2-9B the value before rescaling is 53.77 and the displayed value is 34.10.

Reading the two Gemma scores

Scoring mode and rescaling are what separate the two Gemma scores. The figure sets the published results side by side with three pieces of procedure a reader would need in order to compare them. None of the three is a field in either source. Under each dashed box is what we recovered from the leaderboard's About page and the report's text.

The same pattern holds for other models. For Gemma-7B, Gemma-2-2B and MAmmoTH2-7B-Plus, the gap between the leaderboard's unrescaled value and a published paper result is between 4 and 21 pp, and rescaling accounts for the rest.

Likelihood ranking does not always score far below generation. On the full MMLU test set we scored four models both ways and the results differ by at most 0.21 pp. The BBH gap comes from applying likelihood ranking to a benchmark built for chain-of-thought answers, with the rescaling on top.

The parser is part of the score

In generation mode the parser is easy to overlook, because when it works you never see it. Our clearest example comes from GSM8K, a set of grade-school arithmetic problems. Its reference solutions end with the delimiter #### followed by the number, and lm-evaluation-harness finds the model's answer by searching for the same delimiter.

We ran Qwen2.5-14B-Instruct on 200 GSM8K problems under three prompts that differ only in the system message, with the same harness, the same five worked examples and temperature 0. With the model's default system message it scores 58.5%. With an instruction to answer carefully and directly it scores 57.0%. With an instruction to think step by step it scores 2.5%. The figure shows one problem under the first and third prompts.

The arithmetic is fine. Told to show its reasoning, the model writes the solution as prose and leaves the delimiter out, and the parser records correct answers as invalid. The same thing happens with no worked examples: every zero-shot GSM8K run in our experiments scores exactly 0.0, because nothing has shown the model the delimiter.

Other models behave differently. In a replication on the full test set, run with a different inference backend, Mistral-7B kept the delimiter under all three prompts and scored 38.4%, 41.0% and 40.4%. The effect belongs to a particular model, prompt and parser together, which is why none of the three can be left out of a description of the score.

To compare this effect with the other choices a harness makes, we held the model and the benchmark fixed and changed one setting at a time. The main grid ran four instruction-tuned models, between 7B and 14B parameters, with lm-evaluation-harness 0.4.11 on 200-item subsets; further runs covered four more multiple-choice benchmarks. In the table, each cell is how far the score moves on average when exactly one setting differs between two otherwise identical runs. The last column compares two random seeds, which gives the noise floor.

On GSM8K and MMLU, a change of prompt format moves the score by many times the seed-to-seed change. On WinoGrande and OpenBookQA it barely exceeds it. Temperature stays close to the noise floor. Put as shares of the total variation in five-shot GSM8K scores, prompt format accounts for about 27% (bootstrap 95% interval 14.4% to 44.9%) and temperature for 0.25%. Both large GSM8K cells are largely the parser failure from the previous figure, seen in aggregate.

A small effect of temperature on the score says nothing about which text was sampled. We would not carry this result over to uses that depend on the diversity of outputs.

A parser can also change underneath a published number. In February 2025 the Open LLM Leaderboard replaced the answer parser it used for MATH Level 5. Scores rose by 4.66 pp on average across 3,751 models, and by about 90 pp in the most extreme cases. Its records carry a submission date but no evaluation timestamp and no parser version, so a record does not say which parser produced its score.

When a model does the judging

For open-ended tasks there is no reference answer to match, and a second model is often asked to rate the output. The judge is then part of the measurement in the same way a parser is. To see how much, we had five models each answer the 80 first-turn questions of MT-Bench once, and gave the same answers to four judge models from different providers, each using the standard 1 to 10 rubric at temperature 0. The answers are fixed, so any change in a model's mean rating comes from the judge.

Across the four judges a model's mean moves by 1.08 points on average and by up to 1.35. Two of the judges order the five models identically, and another pair disagree more than they agree (rank correlation −0.30). Some of the differences between neighbouring models under one judge are under a tenth of a point, so we would not read much into any single crossing. What the chart does show is that a rating has no meaning without the name of the judge. The name is still not enough: the judge's prompt and the rule that turns its response into a number are part of the measurement as well.

The same benchmark name, different procedures

We followed up several more large gaps between two sources reporting the same model on the same benchmark, most of them above 30 pp, tracing each by hand through documentation, methods sections and citations. We chose cases we expected to be able to diagnose, so they show what kinds of cause exist and say nothing about how common each is. Each row below is one model on one benchmark, and each dot is a published score on a line from 0 to 100.

The sources for these cases are RecurrentGemma and other model papers for Gemma-7B; Instella for Gemma-2-2B; MAmmoTH2; Belebele as reported by Reka and Falcon2; TruthfulQA as reported in the Code Llama and OLMo papers; HellaSwag as reported by Phi-3 and Gemma; and SEA-LION with the Gemma 3 report.

The first group is what the previous section explained: scoring mode and rescaling, neither written down. The second is a different problem. A benchmark name such as Belebele or TruthfulQA covers several legitimate ways of running it, with different shot counts, language subsets or judges, and the name alone does not say which. The third is the opposite of a disagreement. Two sources carry the same 34.10, which looks like one confirming the other, and is one measurement reported twice.

Where we found a cause, it was because documentation happened to survive somewhere outside the record: an About page, a methods section, or a citation for a reused number. For Gemma-7B on HellaSwag nothing of the kind survived, and the gap stays unexplained.

What published records keep

The cases above turned on scoring mode, harness, prompt, shot count, parser and judge. To see how often details like these are published, we collected 46,559 evaluation records covering 7,398 models and 1,954 benchmarks. Ten sources are leaderboards or structured repositories. The other two are 100 arXiv model papers and technical reports from 2022 to 2026, and Papers With Code.

For each record we checked the five details of the procedure that our harmonized records can hold: shot count, harness, chain-of-thought status, prompt template and temperature. In the chart, compare the middle column with the right-hand one. The only difference is one leaderboard, the one whose values we mostly filled in ourselves, and without it four of the five rows are empty.

The harness row shows why coverage cannot be read at face value. Across all records it stands at 63.2%. But 57.5 of those points are Open LLM Leaderboard v2 records to which we attached the harness name ourselves, because its About page names it. None of the ten leaderboard APIs returns a harness field. Leaderboard shot counts and chain-of-thought status come mostly from the same kind of inference: each platform runs one fixed protocol, and we filled the value in from its documentation.

The arXiv records were extracted from papers with a language model. Against hand-checked tables from ten papers the extracted scores are 97.5% precise, with 81.4% recall. See Details and controls.

That one leaderboard contributes 26,790 of the 28,755 leaderboard records. Without it, four of the five fields are empty, and shot count survives at 71.0% only for leaderboards that run at a single fixed shot count.

The leaderboard's own row for google/gemma-2-9b has 36 columns. It identifies the evaluated model precisely, down to the exact weight revision, the numeric precision and whether a chat template was applied, and it gives every benchmark both a raw and a displayed value. No column names the harness, the scoring mode, the prompt template or the temperature. When the score is repeated in a comparison table or on another site, most of even this is dropped: what travels is little more than the model name and the six displayed scores.

How often can one source be checked against another?

If records do not say how a score was produced, the next best check is a second source reporting the same model on the same benchmark. The 46,559 records cover 41,999 distinct pairs of model and benchmark. Under our name matching, 291 of those pairs appear in at least two sources.

A cross-check is possible for fewer than one pair in a hundred, and where it is possible the sources usually disagree. The registry lists the pairs in the current datastore that more than one source reports. For 98% of these disagreements the harness is unknown, and the scoring mode is never recorded. Agreement is not always reassuring either. As the Gemma case showed, two sources can carry the same number because one took it from the other.

One regularity is visible even without metadata. Open LLM Leaderboard v2 scores below a paper reporting the same model in 37 of 42 BBH comparisons, with a median gap of 25.6 pp, and in 19 of 24 MMLU-Pro comparisons, with a median gap of 8.0 pp. Each comparison sets the leaderboard against whichever result a paper chose to report, so these gaps mix differences of protocol with differences in what gets reported, and should not be read as the size of any one effect.

EvalSpec: fields to publish with a score

Metadata that was left out cannot be recovered after the fact, so a remedy has to apply to results published from now on. EvalSpec is our proposal for the minimum a published score should carry. It is a conformance profile, a statement of which fields must be present, over the Every Eval Ever schema as we extended it for this study (version 0.2.1 plus eight fields). It adds no file format of its own: most EvalSpec fields name a location that already exists in that schema and make it mandatory. One field is new, scoring_mode, because no existing path carries it.

This section explains the fields with the cases that motivate them. The field list with types and rules, the JSON Schema and worked examples are on the Spec page; the Submit page checks a record in the browser.

The fields come in three tiers. Required fields let a reader decide whether two scores were produced under the same regime. Recommended fields are what you would need to re-run the evaluation. Full fields support an audit. Where we have evidence, the tiers follow it: prompt and shot count moved scores by tens of points and are Required, while temperature barely moved them and is Recommended.

The four values of scoring_mode are set by how the reported number is produced. log-likelihood: the model assigns a probability to each answer option given in advance and the most probable is selected; no text is generated. generation: the model writes free text and a parser extracts the answer. preference: a human or model judge rates or ranks outputs, and the score is derived from those judgments. execution: the output is run against test cases or a verifier. A pipeline with several stages is labelled by the stage that produces the number.

EvalSpec is a proposal and has not been validated as a standard. No record we collected satisfies the Required tier, because its fields are the ones public sources tend to leave out.

The hook's record is not yet a complete Required-tier record. It fills seven of the thirteen Required fields and two Recommended ones, and reads scoring_mode from the task's output_type. It lacks two things. It has no answer_parser_version, which a generation-scored result needs. Its hash covers only the task's prompt template, where a conformant hash also covers the system instruction and chat template. That gap matters: our three GSM8K prompts differ only in the system message, so this hash would not tell the 58.5% run from the 2.5% run.

We show the hook's output anyway because of where its information comes from. A harness holds the full configuration in memory when it computes a score, so writing it out needs no extra measurement, and large or sensitive items such as prompts can be recorded as hashes. That makes harnesses the natural place to start. Model developers can publish the resulting records next to model cards and technical reports. Leaderboards can expose the Required fields through their APIs, where today they are implicit in the platform's protocol, and keep the source of any score they import so that a copy is not counted as a replication.

Details and controls

How the corpus was built

Leaderboards alone could not support the cross-source analysis, since few model and benchmark pairs appear on more than one of them. We therefore added 100 arXiv model papers and extracted their result tables with a language model. 17,228 of the 46,559 records, 37%, come from that extraction. Against hand-checked tables from ten papers, 1,977 of 2,027 extracted triples matched (97.5% precision) at 81.4% recall. Settings are harder than scores: in a separate six-paper audit, carried out with a second model, 42.3% of the settings our pipeline had filled in were never stated by the paper and are defaults or guesses. For harness names the extractor erred on the cautious side, returning "unknown" for 105 of 368 rows where the paper did name one, and never naming a harness the paper did not.

Settings that moved scores little

Three further results belong next to the table of score changes. A separate sweep of sampling temperature from 0.0 to 1.0 accounts for 2.2% of the variance in GSM8K accuracy and 0.66% in MT-Bench judged scores, where the mean spread is 0.05 on the ten-point scale. Two releases of one harness, lm-evaluation-harness 0.4.3 and 0.4.11, differ by at most 1.97 pp on GSM8K, less than the spread between two seeds (up to 2.81 pp). And on MMLU, zero-shot and five-shot means pooled over runs are 60.17 and 61.16, a difference that is not significant, even though individual pairs of runs move by 6.3 pp on average in one direction or the other.

The parser effect on GSM8K also depends on the software around the model. In a replication on the full test set with a different inference backend, the two Qwen2.5 models fell from 37.0% to 7.0% and from 38.2% to 0.1% between the plain and chain-of-thought prompts. A pooled test over three models there was underpowered, and a further run on the same backend with a different few-shot seed and batch size reversed the ordering for one of the models. We cannot rule out that the backend itself shifts scores.

Related proposals

EvalSpec sits beside several efforts to document evaluations, among them model cards, Evaluation Cards, Eval Factsheets and Croissant Tasks. Those describe an evaluation for a human reader or define a general metadata format. EvalSpec is narrower: a fixed list of machine-readable fields, chosen by which ones were missing in our audit and moved scores in our experiments.

What this does not show

Our coverage numbers count inferred and default values, so they overstate what leaderboards publish, while extraction misses pull the other way for papers. Matching records across sources depends on heuristic name normalization. Matches we missed make 0.69% a lower bound on overlap. Wrong merges are the worse error: two different checkpoints collapsed into one name appear as a disagreement that our own pipeline created.

One platform supplies 93% of the leaderboard records, so the aggregate statistics mostly describe the Open LLM Leaderboard v2. The cases we traced were chosen because we thought they could be diagnosed, and one of them could not. The controlled experiments use four instruction-tuned models between 7B and 14B parameters, English benchmarks and subsampled test sets, with few independent units per test.

EvalSpec makes protocols visible. It does not make them the same: two fully documented scores produced by different procedures are still not comparable, and the record only lets you see that. It cannot repair the historical records examined here. And we have not measured what it costs a harness or a leaderboard to adopt it.
